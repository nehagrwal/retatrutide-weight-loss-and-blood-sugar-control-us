What Is Retatrutide? Weight Loss & Blood Sugar Control

Retatrutide (development code LY3437943) is an investigational drug developed by Eli Lilly. It's an investigational weekly injection in trials for obesity, overweight-related health issues and type 2 diabetes. Unlike other medications that only target one or two metabolic hormone receptors, retatrutide activates three: the GIP, GLP-1 and glucagon receptors.

These hormone pathways affect appetite, digestion, insulin secretion and energy metabolism. Retatrutide is designed to impact all three processes that control body weight and glucose regulation. Researchers are exploring whether this combination of action can offer significant benefits to people who have struggled to achieve lasting results with lifestyle changes alone.

Retatrutide is currently in Phase 3 clinical testing for weight loss and blood sugar control, with results expected in 2026 and anticipated to show a dramatic difference in body weight and A1C for certain study populations. However, retatrutide is still experimental and not approved by the U.S. or Canadian governments for regular public use. Clinical trial results should not be assumed to predict individual results.

GIP activity also participates in the regulation of glucose and glucagon receptor activity may influence energy expenditure and the handling of energy stores. Their combined effects are being explored to assess their overall clinical utility.

Retatrutide Benefits for Weight Loss and Blood Sugar Control

The main potential benefit is significant weight saving. In the Phase 3 TRIUMPH-1 trial in adults with obesity or overweight without diabetes, at 80 weeks the 12 mg dose resulted in an average weight loss of ~28.3% from baseline body weight. This is a group average in the conditions of the study, not a prediction for each patient. Outcomes can differ based on dosage, compliance, tolerance and personal health considerations.

Retatrutide Weight Loss and Blood Sugar Control Risks and Side Effects

The most frequent side effects observed in clinical trials were gastrointestinal symptoms including nausea, diarrhoea, vomiting, constipation and reduced appetite. These effects may be more severe during dose escalation and may affect hydration, food intake and ability to continue treatment. Side effects cause some of the participants to stop treatment. Therefore, the large average weight losses reported in trials need to be interpreted in the light of tolerability.

Another big issue is potential gallbladder problems, kidney issues from dehydration and pancreatitis. The full risk profile remains under investigation. Retatrutide is not approved and therefore has no official prescribing information, contraindications, and long-term safety profile for routine clinical use. Potential risks should not be assumed to be the same as other drugs that act on GLP-1 or related hormone pathways.

Especially low blood sugar is a concern if a glucose-lowering medication is given together with other medications that can cause a low blood sugar, such as insulin or sulfonylureas. People with diabetes should not, on their own, change doses of existing medications in anticipation of retatrutide. Products marketed as research peptides or compounded retatrutide outside of approved clinical research have added risks associated with unknown concentration, sterility, dosing and purity. These products should not be considered safe alternatives to a regulated medicine.
